You've been burned before. A freelancer ghosted after Apple rejected your app. Another one shipped something that crashed on day one. A third one said they "definitely handle rejections" and then disappeared when it actually happened.

Now you're scared to hire anyone.

Here's the thing: **You're not scared to hire. You're scared to hire the wrong person.** And I can teach you how to spot them in 5 minutes.

There are exactly 5 questions that separate developers who can actually ship apps from developers who say they can.

## The 5 Questions That Matter

Ask these before you sign anything.

### Question 1: "How Many Apps Have You Shipped to Both App Stores?"

This is the filter question. If the answer is less than 5, stop here.

Someone who's shipped 5+ apps has seen the Apple review process under pressure. They've seen rejections. They've learned what works and what doesn't. They have pattern recognition.

Someone who's shipped 1–2 apps is still learning. They got lucky on their last one. Or they only shipped to Android, which is a completely different process than Apple's. Or they built the app themselves and had someone else handle submission—they think they know what they're doing, but they don't.

**Red flags:**
- "I've shipped 3 apps" (not enough data)
- "I'm a full-stack developer, I ship apps all the time" (vague—how many? to what stores?)
- "I've shipped web apps and mobile apps" (web apps are irrelevant to app store shipping)
- "I've never shipped one, but I've built lots of apps" (building ≠ shipping)

**Green flag:**
- "I've shipped 12 apps to both iOS and Android in the last 3 years"

### Question 2: "Show Me an App You Shipped That Got Rejected. What Was the Reason? How Did You Appeal It?"

This is the reality check.

If they've never been rejected by Apple, they haven't shipped much. Apple rejects apps for a reason. The developers who succeed are the ones who've been rejected, understood why, and fixed it.

The best answer here is detailed: "We shipped an app that Apple rejected for 4.2 (minimum functionality). We'd built a great UI but the core feature wasn't working on iPad. We fixed the iPad layout, appealed with detailed notes about what we changed, and got approved on the second try."

That's someone who understands the process.

**Red flags:**
- "I've never had an app rejected" (either 1–2 apps or they're lying)
- "Apple rejected it but I don't remember why" (they didn't care enough to remember)
- "I just resubmitted and it got approved" (no plan, just luck)
- "I don't handle rejections; the app owner does" (they're avoiding responsibility)
- "Apple is unfair; they rejected it for no reason" (they don't understand Apple's guidelines)

**Green flag:**
- "We got a 4.2 for unclear functionality. We submitted a video showing the feature working, added clearer onboarding, and appealed with a detailed explanation. Approved on resubmit."

### Question 3: "Can You Guarantee Approval, or Do You Charge Extra for Appeals?"

This one cuts straight to incentive alignment.

If they charge hourly and rejections happen, they make more money. If they have a flat fee with rejection appeals included, they make the same money either way. Which one do you want?

The answer should be: "Rejections are included in my scope. If Apple rejects, I fix it for free. You pay once; I handle everything until it's approved."

**Red flags:**
- "Approval isn't guaranteed, so I charge hourly" (you'll pay for every rejection)
- "Each resubmission is an extra `$500`" (they're incentivized to dismiss your concerns)
- "That depends on the reason for the rejection" (vague—they'll charge you on a case-by-case basis)
- "I'll do the first resubmit, but after that you're on your own" (they're checking out)

**Green flag:**
- "Approval and one round of appeals are included. If it takes more than that, we talk about next steps, but rejections happen and I handle them as part of the service."

### Question 4: "What Happens If the App Crashes or Has Bugs After Approval?"

This one catches people who don't think about the full lifecycle.

The answer should be: "If bugs appear after approval because of something I did, I fix them for free. I stand behind my work. If you add a feature after launch and break something, that's a separate request, but launch bugs are on me."

**Red flags:**
- "After launch, that's your problem" (no accountability)
- "I'm not a QA team; you should test it" (avoids responsibility)
- "Bugs cost extra to fix" (they're not confident in their work)
- "That's usually the app owner's job" (they're passing the buck)

**Green flag:**
- "I test thoroughly before submission. If something crashes after launch because of my code, I fix it immediately. That's part of the job."

### Question 5: "Can You Provide References From Real Founders Who've Shipped With You?"

References from other developers are nice. References from founders who actually paid for the service and shipped an app are gold.

When you call these references, ask:
- "Did the app get approved on the first try?"
- "How long did it take from start to approval?"
- "What happened when Apple rejected? How did they handle it?"
- "Would you hire them again?"

**Red flags:**
- No references available
- References are other developers, not founders/product owners
- References are all from 5+ years ago
- References won't answer questions about rejection handling

**Green flag:**
- Multiple references from the last 12 months
- Founders who speak specifically about rejection handling
- References who'd hire them again

## The Red Flags That Kill It Immediately

If you hear any of these, stop interviewing:

**"App Store approval is easy."** No developer who's shipped 10+ apps says this. Easy developers who've shipped 1–2 apps say this. Apple's review process is arbitrary, slow, and demanding. Anyone who minimizes it hasn't done it at scale.

**"We'll just wrap your web app in a native shell (Capacitor, React Native, Flutter wrapper)."** Listen, wrappers are fine for certain things. But "just wrap it" as a shipping strategy means they don't understand what Apple actually approves. Wrappers fail review for "minimal functionality" or "web apps aren't welcome here" constantly. Someone who leads with this hasn't shipped wrapped apps to Apple and had them approved.

**"We can ship it in 3 days."** Shipping takes time. Testing takes time. The App Store review queue is 1–2 weeks minimum. If someone guarantees faster, they're either lying or they're rushing it (and it'll get rejected).

**"We use offshore development / outsource the submission."** This is a non-answer. Where does the submission happen? Who's accountable if Apple rejects? If they can't answer this clearly, they don't own the process.

**"I focus on the app; the store stuff is a separate concern."** Building and shipping are different skills. Someone who says this is good at coding. They're not good at shipping. Hire someone who owns the whole thing.

## The Green Flags That Matter

**They've shipped 10+ apps.** This is the baseline. 10+ ships means they've seen the patterns. They know what Apple actually cares about.

**They can show a rejection and explain the appeal.** Not a generic rejection. A specific one: "4.2 for minimum functionality" or "3.1.1 for business model clarity" or "5.1.1 for data privacy." And they can tell you what they did to appeal.

**Flat-fee pricing with appeals included.** This is the signal that they're confident and aligned with you. You pay once. They handle rejections. Simple.

**They give you a timeline with caveats.** "3–4 weeks from code handoff to approval, assuming no rejections. If Apple rejects, each resubmit adds 1–2 weeks." That's honest.

**They have recent references.** Not from 5 years ago. From the last 6–12 months. From founders, not other developers.

**They ask you clarifying questions.** "Is this a free app or paid? Do you use in-app purchases? Do you collect personal data? Is this US-only or international?" The right person wants to know what they're shipping before they quote you.

## What to Do With the Answers

When you get the answers:

1. **Score them.** Green flags = +1. Red flags = -1. If they're under 0 after all 5 questions, pass.

2. **Check references.** Don't skip this. A reference call takes 15 minutes. It's the difference between hiring someone good and hiring someone who ghosted on the last 3 projects.

3. **Trust your gut on communication.** If someone isn't responsive during the vetting process, they won't be responsive during the project. Slow response now = slow response on rejections. That costs you time.

4. **Ask about edge cases.** "What if Apple rejects for privacy reasons? What if we need to appeal? What if we need a feature added after submission?" Watch how they handle curveballs.

The developers who get defensive or vague? Pass. The ones who lean in and explain the process? Hire those ones.
